# 01 — Master Game Bible

## 1. High concept
THA T.R.A.P. is a 2D 16-bit urban cyber RPG. The player joins THE SWITCHBOARD, a community-rooted cyber defense collective protecting New Meridian's people, institutions and infrastructure from THE GHOST NETWORK: a loosely coordinated criminal ecosystem exploiting weak identity controls, vulnerable vendors, poor security operations, data exposure and AI-enabled workflows.

The game blends:
- Exploration and character-driven story.
- Career progression and practical cybersecurity training.
- Case files, evidence boards and incident command.
- A fictional SIEM, endpoint console, network map, identity portal and safe terminal.
- Branching decisions with consequences for trust, service availability, privacy and recovery.

## 2. Genre and player fantasy
**Genre:** Story-driven RPG + investigative simulation + cybersecurity learning game.  
**Player fantasy:** Become the defender people call when the systems they depend on are in trouble.

The player is not a lone hacker superhero. They are an analyst who listens, verifies, collaborates, documents and makes careful decisions.

## 3. Core loop
1. **Hear the community:** receive a report, referral, alert or service complaint.
2. **Frame the problem:** identify assets, users, business impact, scope and known facts.
3. **Learn the tool:** receive a short lesson or guided demonstration.
4. **Investigate:** query synthetic logs, inspect endpoint/network/identity artifacts and build a timeline.
5. **Decide:** classify, escalate, contain, preserve evidence or monitor.
6. **Communicate:** write a ticket, stakeholder update, incident summary or handoff.
7. **Validate:** complete a knowledge check and practical assessment.
8. **Reflect:** receive rubric-based feedback, skill progress and a new lead in the story.

## 4. Pillars and success measures
| Pillar | Player experience | Acceptance evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Authentic learning | Skills taught in plain English then practiced | Every mission has objectives, practice and assessment |
| Evidence-led defense | Conclusions must cite artifacts | Rubric checks evidence IDs and reasoning |
| Community impact | Security protects real human needs | Mission outcomes show service and trust effects |
| Career progression | Skills unlock responsibilities | Skill graph tied to observable competencies |
| Premium retro presentation | Cohesive pixel-art world and SOC UI | Art bible and UI tokens followed |
| Safe simulation | No uncontrolled external actions | Scenario engine cannot access host/network |

## 5. Audience and progression
- New learner: no prior experience; guided controls, glossary, visual metaphors.
- Foundation learner: basic networking, operating systems, accounts and logs.
- Junior analyst: alert triage, SIEM queries, endpoint and identity investigations.
- Developing practitioner: threat hunting, incident response, vulnerability and app/cloud security.
- Specialist track: AI security, malware triage, OT/ICS, security architecture and automation.

The game should not imply that finishing all content makes someone universally job-ready. The portfolio shows what was practiced, at what difficulty, and against which rubric.

## 6. Player avatar
Player chooses name, pronouns (optional), skin tone, hair, clothing palette and accessibility preferences. No identity option changes stats or dialogue outcomes. Cosmetic choices are not locked behind paywalls.

## 7. Core cast
- **Nia “Patch” Brooks — SOC lead:** patient, exacting, funny when the room needs air; teaches evidence discipline.
- **Malik “M-Stack” Carter — systems engineer:** practical Windows/Linux and identity specialist; explains how systems actually behave.
- **Imani Reed — incident commander:** calm under pressure; teaches prioritization, communication and impact assessment.
- **Dre “Packet” Johnson — network analyst:** curious, playful, excellent at turning traffic patterns into stories.
- **Auntie Rochelle — neighborhood business association organizer:** community voice; challenges the team to explain risk without jargon.
- **Dr. Simone Ellis — hospital security liaison:** emphasizes patient safety, availability and careful change control.
- **Jules Park — AI assurance engineer:** helps the team assess model, retrieval, tool and agent risks.
- **Director Vale — city technology executive:** not a villain; represents budget, policy, public accountability and trade-offs.

Characters must have distinct goals, flaws, expertise, relationships and arcs. Avoid making every character a walking lesson. They have lives outside the plot.

## 8. Antagonist structure
**THE GHOST NETWORK** is not one omnipotent hacker. It is a criminal ecosystem with brokers, access resellers, fraud crews, opportunistic affiliates, compromised vendors and automation operators. Its strength comes from fragmentation and weak coordination among victims.

The recurring antagonist known as **The Curator** organizes stolen access and data into packages. The player gradually discovers that the biggest weakness is not one magic exploit; it is the repeated failure to manage identity, vendors, logging, recovery and accountability.

Do not glorify attackers or present attacks as effortless. Show operational friction, mistakes, detection, attribution uncertainty and consequences.

## 9. Progression
- **Trust:** community confidence and stakeholder cooperation.
- **Skill XP:** practice progress, never a substitute for assessment.
- **Competencies:** independently assessed abilities.
- **Reputation:** reliability, communication and ethical conduct.
- **Resources:** limited team capacity, time windows and service impact.
- **Case portfolio:** evidence-backed work samples.

Unlocks should be earned through competencies, not repetitive grinding. Replaying a mission can improve mastery without farming unlimited XP.

## 10. Consequence model
Track:
- Community trust.
- Service availability.
- Data confidentiality.
- Evidence integrity.
- Response cost.
- Team fatigue.
- Organizational resilience.

A high score requires a defensible balance. A dramatic but reckless action should not automatically be rewarded.

## 11. Content rating and tone
Target a broad teen/adult learning audience. Depict fraud, ransomware, harassment, privacy loss and service disruption without graphic violence or exploitative victim imagery. Dialogue can be funny, warm, local and culturally grounded without forced slang.

## 12. Replayability
- Optional evidence artifacts.
- Alternative valid response paths.
- Different hints and tutorial depth.
- Scenario variants with changed timestamps, identities and telemetry.
- Challenge mode with reduced guidance.
- Post-mission “what else could have happened?” debrief.

## 13. Monetization recommendation
Prefer a transparent paid game or free core + clearly disclosed optional expansion. No loot boxes, pay-to-win skill boosts, paid access to essential learning, or manipulative streaks. All core curriculum should remain accessible offline.
